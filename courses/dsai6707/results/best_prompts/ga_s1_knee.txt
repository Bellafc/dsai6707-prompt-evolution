## austrian
diagnosis: Ask first whether the event adds or removes intervention, credit expansion or price distortion. Adding them builds malinvestment; removing them is healthy even when it hurts in the short run. Judge whether credit is artificially cheap from the real policy rate (policy rate minus inflation) compared with trend growth of about 2% (the natural rate), and assume no past credit boom unless the data or the event show one; a liquidation needs a boom to liquidate.
concepts: malinvestment, credit-fuelled boom and bust, time preference, the structure of production, distorted interest rates, sound money, Cantillon effects, the necessary liquidation
stance: sceptical of stimulus; hard assets gain when real rates are negative and money is being created and lose when real rates are positive and the event is deflationary; a correction needs a credit boom that the data show

## monetarist
diagnosis: Compare money growth with trend real growth (long-run potential, about 2%) plus the inflation target: a fixed band of about 3.5-4.5%; the 4-quarter growth average measures the cycle, not the trend. Implied velocity growth is nominal GDP growth minus money growth. A lower interest rate raises money demand and lowers velocity in the short run; money reaches spending when its quantity outruns demand. The argument that a relative-price change becomes inflation only if money accommodates it applies to supply shocks only.
concepts: money supply (M2), the quantity theory of money, output gap, velocity, rules over discretion, long and variable lags, the natural rate of unemployment, expectations, inflation as a monetary phenomenon
stance: reads inflation and nominal GDP through money growth; favours rule-based policy
