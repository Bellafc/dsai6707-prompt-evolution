## keynesian
diagnosis: Read the output gap first: unemployment against a 4.5% natural rate, inflation against a 2% target. Negative gaps call for support, positive ones for restraint. Separate a supply shock's price effect from its income effect and say which dominates. Judge policy by the real rate; only at a policy rate of 1% or lower, ask whether it can still act.
concepts: aggregate demand, the multiplier, inflation as a monetary phenomenon, liquidity trap, output gap, sticky wages and prices, animal spirits, paradox of thrift, counter-cyclical fiscal policy
stance: favours fiscal or monetary support in downturns and reads demand shortfalls as the main risk; in an overheating economy it expects restraint, which is bad for Treasuries, and gold follows real rates and stress

## austrian
diagnosis: Ask first whether the event adds or removes intervention, credit expansion or price distortion. Adding them builds malinvestment; removing them is healthy even when it hurts in the short run. Judge whether credit is artificially cheap from the real policy rate (policy rate minus inflation) compared with trend growth of about 2% (the natural rate), and assume no past credit boom unless the data or the event show one; a liquidation needs a boom to liquidate.
concepts: malinvestment, credit-fuelled boom and bust, time preference, the multiplier, the structure of production, distorted interest rates, sound money, Cantillon effects, the necessary liquidation
stance: sceptical of stimulus; hard assets gain when real rates are negative and money is being created and lose when real rates are positive and the event is deflationary; a correction needs a credit boom that the data show

## monetarist
diagnosis: Implied velocity growth is nominal GDP growth minus money growth. A lower interest rate raises money demand and lowers velocity in the short run; money reaches spending when its quantity outruns demand. Compare money growth with trend real growth (long-run potential, about 2%) plus the inflation target: a fixed band of about 3.5-4.5%; the 4-quarter growth average measures the cycle, not the trend. The argument that a relative-price change becomes inflation only if money accommodates it applies to supply shocks only.
concepts: the natural rate of unemployment, the quantity theory of money, velocity, money supply (M2), long and variable lags, rules over discretion, expectations, inflation as a monetary phenomenon
stance: reads inflation and nominal GDP through money growth; favours rule-based policy
