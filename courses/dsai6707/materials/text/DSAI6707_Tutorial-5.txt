Multi-objective problems

 A multi-objective optimization problem (MOP) has at least two objectives.



   minimize f(x) = [f₁(x), f₂(x), …, fₘ(x)]
   subject to x ∈ Ω,          m≥2

 x: decision variables     Ω: feasible set

 The objectives may conflict, so several trade-off solutions can be useful.

Conflicting objectives

 Example: choosing a model for deployment

                Model                 Latency (ms) ↓   Error (%) ↓
                  A                            10          12
                  B                            20          8
                  C                            35          5
                  D                            25          10

 A is faster than B, but B is more accurate.
 B improves both objectives relative to D.

Pareto dominance

 For minimization, a dominates b (a ≺ b) when:

   fⱼ(a) ≤ fⱼ(b) for every objective j
   and fⱼ(a) < fⱼ(b) for at least one objective.


                 Comparison                               Conclusion
            B = (20, 8), D = (25, 10)                    B dominates D
            A = (10, 12), B = (20, 8)              Neither dominates the other

Evolutionary search

 Multi-objective Evolutionary Algorithms (MOEAs)

 A population explores several trade-offs in one search run.


 Selection encourages convergence towards better objective values.


 Diversity preservation maintains alternatives across the front.



 MOGA and NSGA-II are genetic-algorithm-based MOEAs.
                                                                     7
The MOEA cycle

 1. Initialize a population of candidate decisions.

 2. Evaluate every objective for each candidate.

 3. Select parents using objective quality and diversity.

 4. Apply crossover and mutation to generate offspring.

 5. Form the next population and repeat until termination.


 Output: a non-dominated approximation set for subsequent decision making.
                                                                             8
MOGA: algorithm

 Multi-objective Genetic Algorithm (Fonseca–Fleming, 1993)

 1. Initialize and evaluate the population.

 2. Count dominators and assign a rank to each individual.

 3. Assign rank-based fitness and apply fitness sharing.

 4. Select parents in proportion to their adjusted fitness.

 5. Generate and evaluate offspring using crossover and mutation.

 6. Replace the population and repeat until the stopping criterion.


                                                                      9
MOGA: rank and fitness

 Dominance-count rank

    r(x) = 1 + |{y ∈ P : y ≺ x}|
 A non-dominated individual has rank 1. Lower ranks are better.


 Fitness assignment
 1. Sort individuals by rank and assign decreasing position scores.

 2. Average scores within each rank to obtain equal base fitness.


                                                                      10
MOGA: fitness sharing

 Sharing reduces the reproductive advantage of crowded solutions.


  nᵢ = Σⱼ sh(dᵢⱼ)           and         F′ᵢ = Fᵢ / nᵢ

  sh(d) = max(0, 1 − d / σ)

 nᵢ measures crowding among individuals of the same rank, including itself.

 Rescale within each rank to preserve that rank’s total base fitness.

 The sharing radius σ and objective scaling affect the result.

Solution: fitness sharing

 Rank 1 has total base fitness 5 ×6 = 30.

     Candidates         Base Fᵢ       Niche count nᵢ    Shared F′ᵢ   Rescaled fitness

       A, D, E             6                1               6            7.5 each

 Shared total = 3 ×6 + 2 ×3 = 24.

 Rescaling factor = 30 / 24 = 1.25.

 A, D and E each receive twice the selection weight of B or C.

NSGA-II: algorithm

 Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II

 1. Initialize N parents P₀, then assign front ranks and crowding distances.

 2. Select parents, apply crossover and mutation, and evaluate N offspring Qₜ.

 3. Combine parents and offspring: Rₜ = Pₜ ∪ Qₜ, with size 2N.

 4. Sort Rₜ into fronts and calculate crowding distances within each front.

 5. Select N survivors to form Pₜ₊₁. Repeat steps 2–5.



                                                                                 16
Non-dominated sorting

 1. F₁ contains every individual with no dominator in the current set.


 2. Remove F₁. The non-dominated individuals that remain form F₂.


 3. Continue until every individual belongs to a front.



 NSGA-II rank = front index.
 Fast implementation tracks each individual’s dominator count
 and the set of individuals it dominates.
                                                                         17
Crowding distance

 Within one front, sort individuals separately by each objective.


  CD(i) = Σⱼ [fⱼ(i⁺) − fⱼ(i⁻)] / [max fⱼ − min fⱼ]

  i⁻ and i⁺ are the adjacent individuals in the ordering for objective j.


 Give boundary individuals infinite distance (∞).

 Larger crowding distance favours a less crowded region.

 Use the range within this front. Skip an objective with zero range.

                                                                            18
Selection in NSGA-II

 Parent selection

 Binary tournament:
 1. Prefer the lower front rank.
 2. For equal ranks, prefer larger CD.
 3. Break a remaining tie randomly.

 Survivor selection
 Accept complete fronts in rank order.
 When a front does not fit, take its largest-CD individuals
 until the population contains N survivors.

Key conclusions

 1. A MOP can have several useful trade-off solutions.

 2. Pareto dominance compares solutions without fixed objective weights.

 3. MOGA uses dominance counts and fitness sharing.

 4. NSGA-II uses front ranks, crowding distance and elitist survival.



 A non-dominated population is an approximation, not proof of global optimality.
