ABSTRACT

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) and autonomous agent
frameworks has catalyzed a new paradigm in computational finance. AI agents,
which are systems capable of perceiving financial environments, reasoning over com-
plex data, and executing actions, are being deployed across trading, portfolio man-
agement, risk assessment, fraud detection, and advisory services. This report has
two parts. The first is a survey of AI agents in finance, covering both reinforcement
learning-based agents and LLM-powered autonomous agents. We propose a taxonomy
along two dimensions: agent design (single-agent, multi-agent, memory-augmented,
tool-augmented, and reasoning-and-reflection architectures) and financial application
domain (trading, portfolio management, risk and fraud, advisory, and compliance).
The survey identifies a gap: existing LLM-based financial agents operate primarily
at the individual asset level without explicit macroeconomic transmission-mechanism
reasoning, while macro-focused LLM systems are not directly connected to calibrated
cross-asset allocation signals.
   The second part addresses this gap. We propose Macro-MOSAIC (Mixture-Of-
Schools Analogy-Informed Chains), a four-stage pipeline designed to bridge qualita-
tive macro event interpretation and quantitative asset allocation: (1) dual-channel
retrieval over a historical macro episode database, (2) multi-school causal chain gen-
eration via school-specific fine-tuned LLMs (Keynesian, Austrian, Monetarist), (3)
Bayesian aggregation with regime-conditional weighting and Platt scaling, and (4)
fractional Kelly asset allocation. We report preliminary results on two completed


                                          1
components—Stages 1 and 2—while Stages 3 and 4 remain planned work. First, we
construct a structured database of 141 macro episodes (1971–2025) with 35 FRED
indicators and 22 tracked instruments per episode, yielding 13,571 asset reaction data
points. Second, we fine-tune school-specific LoRA adapters on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
and evaluate on 96 held-out events. Fine-tuning improves school-style vocabulary
consistency by 59.3% on average (Austrian +118.8%, Monetarist +129.7%), reduces
cross-school contamination by up to 100%, and increases inter-school signal diversity
(Jaccard distance 0.853 vs. 0.778 for base); these results are best interpreted as input-
quality validation for Stage 3, not as evidence of end-to-end causal reasoning or trad-
ing performance. We conclude with a detailed plan for the remaining stages, ablation
experiments, and end-to-end evaluation on both reasoning benchmarks (EconCausal,
EconLogicQA) and out-of-sample backtests (2023–2025).

Chapter 1

Introduction

The intersection of AI and finance has a long history, from early expert systems
for credit scoring to statistical arbitrage models powered by machine learning. The
emergence of large language models (LLMs) and autonomous agent frameworks has
introduced a new capability: AI systems that perceive, reason, plan, and act within
complex financial environments with increasing autonomy.
   An AI agent in finance can be defined as a computational system that observes
financial data (prices, news, reports, sentiment), maintains internal state or memory,
reasons about market conditions, and takes actions such as placing trades, generating
reports, or flagging risks. This covers a wide spectrum, from deep reinforcement
learning (DRL) agents trained to optimize portfolio returns [Liu et al., 2022b,a] to
LLM-powered multi-agent frameworks that simulate the collaborative dynamics of
professional trading firms [Xiao et al., 2024].



Part I: Literature Review
Several recent surveys have examined LLM-based financial agents from different an-
gles: Ding et al. [2024] focus specifically on trading applications, Nie et al. [2024]
provide a broad overview of LLMs in financial applications, and Dong et al. [2025]


                                           5
bridge research and real-world deployment. Building on these, the first part of this
report reviews the literature with a specific orientation toward the design of our pro-
posed system: we organize the space jointly along agent architecture (single-agent,
multi-agent, memory-augmented, tool-augmented, reasoning-and-reflection) and fi-
nancial application domain (trading, portfolio management, risk and fraud, advisory,
compliance), so that the design choices made in Macro-MOSAIC can be explicitly
situated against existing patterns. The review is organized around the following
questions:

    RQ1: What data sources and modalities are used by AI agents in finance?

    RQ2: What agent design patterns have been proposed, and how do they differ
     architecturally?

    RQ3: How are these designs applied across different financial domains?

    RQ4: How are financial AI agents evaluated, and what benchmarks exist?

    RQ5: What are the key open challenges and promising future directions?



Part II: Gap, Proposed System, and Preliminary
Results
The survey surfaces a specific gap at the boundary between equity-level agents and
macroeconomic reasoning. Existing systems such as FinGPT [Yang et al., 2023],
TradingAgents [Xiao et al., 2024], and FINCON [Yu et al., 2024] generate asset-level
signals from news, indicators, and fundamentals, but operate primarily at the indi-
vidual asset level without explicit macroeconomic transmission-mechanism reasoning.
Conversely, macro-focused LLM systems such as EconAgent [Li et al., 2024] simulate
or forecast macro dynamics but are not directly connected to calibrated cross-asset
allocation signals; equity analysis systems such as MarketSenseAI [Fatouros et al.,

                                          6
2025] similarly lack explicit macro transmission-mechanism reasoning. Three gaps
motivate this work: (i) no system we are aware of provides explicit, auditable multi-
school parallel reasoning chains in which distinct economic frameworks independently
analyze the same macro event and are formally aggregated; (ii) historical analogy re-
trieval at the macro-episode level is absent from the systems we surveyed; (iii) no
system connects qualitative macro interpretation to calibrated probability distribu-
tions and cross-asset signals in a verified end-to-end pipeline evaluated against both
reasoning benchmarks and realized outcomes.
   To address these gaps, we propose Macro-MOSAIC (Mixture-Of-Schools Analogy-
Informed Chains), a four-stage pipeline:

  1. Historical Analogy Retrieval. Dual-channel retrieval over 141 macro episodes
     (1971–2025), combining semantic similarity over event narratives with Maha-
     lanobis distance over 35-dimensional FRED macro state vectors.

  2. Multi-School Causal Chain Generation. Three school-specific LoRA adapters
     (Keynesian, Austrian, Monetarist) independently interpret the retrieved analo-
     gies and produce structured causal chains mapping transmission mechanisms to
     asset impacts.

  3. Bayesian Aggregation and Calibration.             Regime-conditional weighting
     based on rolling Brier Scores, followed by Platt scaling trained on resolved
     Polymarket and Metaculus questions.

  4. Asset Allocation Signal Generation. Fractional Kelly allocation over 8
     liquid instruments, driven by scenario-conditional return forecasts.

   We report preliminary results on two completed components. The episode database
(Section 4.1) contains 141 episodes across 8 categories and 19 transmission channels,
with 35 FRED indicators and 22 instruments per episode, producing 13,571 asset
reaction data points; 117 episodes additionally have LLM-generated narrative sum-


                                           7
maries and three parallel school-specific interpretations (Keynesian, Austrian, Mone-
tarist). The school-specific adapter evaluation (Section 4.2) fine-tunes LoRA adapters
on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using 500 synthetic agent trajectories per school, and evalu-
ates on 96 held-out events. Fine-tuning yields a 59.3% average gain in school-style vo-
cabulary consistency (Austrian +118.8%, Monetarist +129.7%), reduces cross-school
contamination by 51–100%, raises structured output block presence from 51–81%
to 93–99%, and produces a higher inter-school Jaccard diversity (0.853 vs. 0.778);
these results constitute input-quality validation for Stage 3 rather than evidence of
end-to-end system performance.

Literature Review

2.1     Background

2.1.1    Foundations of AI Agents

An AI agent is typically defined as a system that perceives its environment, maintains
internal state, and takes actions to achieve specified goals [Wooldridge and Jennings,
1995, Russell and Norvig, 2010]. In the context of LLM-based agents, recent frame-
works have formalized the agent loop as a cycle of observation, reasoning (often via
chain-of-thought prompting), action selection (potentially using external tools), and
memory update [Wang et al., 2024a, Xi et al., 2025].
   Key architectural components of modern AI agents include: (1) Perception mod-
ules that process environmental inputs, including text, numerical data, and visual
information; (2) Memory mechanisms encompassing short-term (context window)
and long-term (retrieval-augmented) storage for maintaining state across interactions
[Park et al., 2023]; (3) Reasoning capabilities such as chain-of-thought [Wei et al.,
2022], tree-of-thought [Yao et al., 2023], or reflection-based deliberation; (4) Action
interfaces including tool use, API calls, and code generation [Schick et al., 2023];
and (5) Planning modules that decompose complex goals into subgoals [Huang et al.,



                                          9
2024].


2.1.2     From Quantitative Models to Agents in Finance

The evolution of AI in finance can be traced through several paradigms. Early quanti-
tative finance relied on statistical models such as ARIMA and GARCH for time series
prediction. The rise of machine learning brought random forests, gradient boosting,
and neural networks for tasks like credit scoring, fraud detection, and price prediction
[Dixon et al., 2020].
   Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) represented a significant shift toward agent-
based financial systems. DRL agents learn trading policies by interacting with sim-
ulated market environments, optimizing objectives such as cumulative returns or
Sharpe ratio [Hambly et al., 2023]. The FinRL framework [Liu et al., 2022b,a] estab-
lished the first open-source library for financial DRL, supporting algorithms such as
PPO, A2C, DDPG, SAC, and TD3 across stock trading and portfolio management
tasks. FinRL-Meta [Liu et al., 2022a] further extended this with near-real market
environments and comprehensive benchmarking.
   The most recent paradigm shift involves LLM-powered agents that leverage nat-
ural language understanding and reasoning capabilities. These agents can process
unstructured financial text, reason about market conditions in natural language, and
integrate multiple data modalities [Yang et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2024]. This tran-
sition from numerical-only DRL agents to language-capable autonomous agents con-
stitutes the central focus of this survey.


2.1.3     Multi-Agent Systems

Multi-agent systems (MAS) have a long history in computational finance, from agent-
based market simulation models [LeBaron, 2006] to modern LLM-powered collabo-
rative frameworks. The key insight driving multi-agent approaches is that financial
decision-making in practice involves multiple specialized roles—analysts, traders, risk

                                             10
managers—whose interactions produce better outcomes than any single agent [Xiao
et al., 2024]. Recent multi-agent financial systems draw inspiration from the gen-
erative agents framework [Park et al., 2023] and software development multi-agent
systems [Qian et al., 2024], adapting these architectures to the financial domain with
specialized roles and domain-specific tools.



2.2     Data Sources and Modalities
Financial AI agents operate over a diverse landscape of data sources and modalities.
The richness and variety of the data an agent can access fundamentally shapes its
perception of the market and the quality of its decisions. This chapter surveys the
major data categories used by existing financial agents.


2.2.1    Numerical Market Data

The most fundamental data source for financial agents is numerical market data,
including asset prices (open, high, low, close, adjusted close), trading volumes, and
derived technical indicators such as moving averages, RSI, MACD, and Bollinger
Bands. Nearly all trading agents ingest this data as their primary input. In DRL-
based agents such as those built on FinRL [Liu et al., 2022b], the state representation
typically consists of price features and technical indicators organized as a vector or
matrix over a lookback window. LLM-based agents such as FinMem [Yu et al., 2023]
and FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] additionally convert numerical data into textual
summaries or structured prompts that can be processed by language models.


2.2.2    Textual Financial Data

Textual data constitutes a major advantage of LLM-based agents over traditional
quantitative systems. Key textual sources include:



                                          11
   News Articles and Headlines. Financial news is widely used for sentiment anal-
ysis and event detection. Agents such as TradingAgents [Xiao et al., 2024] employ
dedicated news analyst agents that process and summarize relevant articles. FinGPT
[Yang et al., 2023] fine-tunes LLMs on financial news for sentiment-enhanced stock
movement prediction.
   Financial Reports. Regulatory filings such as SEC Form 10-K and 10-Q pro-
vide detailed company financials, management discussions, and risk disclosures. Kim
et al. [2024] demonstrated that LLMs can analyze financial statements with accu-
racy rivaling professional analysts. Trading agents like FinMem [Yu et al., 2023] and
TradingGPT [Li et al., 2023] incorporate financial reports to enrich their memory and
reasoning.
   Social Media and Forums. Platforms such as Twitter/X, Reddit (e.g., r/wallstreetbets),
and StockTwits provide real-time retail investor sentiment. Multi-agent systems of-
ten include dedicated sentiment analyst agents that process social media feeds [Xiao
et al., 2024].
   Analyst Reports. Professional analyst reports and investment research provide
high-quality expert opinions. FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] incorporates expert guid-
ance from platforms such as SeekingAlpha as a key input to its decision-making
module.


2.2.3     Visual and Chart Data

Financial charts encode important pattern information that is not easily captured
by numerical features alone. FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] pioneered the integration
of visual data in LLM-based trading agents, incorporating Kline charts and trad-
ing charts alongside numerical and textual data. Using multimodal LLMs such as
GPT-4V, FinAgent demonstrated that visual inputs can significantly improve trad-
ing performance compared to text-and-number-only baselines. Despite this promise,
visual financial data remains underexplored relative to text and numerical modali-


                                         12
ties, partly because most LLMs have not been specifically trained on financial chart
interpretation.


2.2.4     Alternative Data

An emerging trend is the incorporation of alternative data sources that go beyond
traditional financial data. These include insider trading records and institutional
holdings, macroeconomic indicators and central bank communications, supply chain
data and satellite imagery, and earnings call audio transcripts. TradingAgents [Xiao
et al., 2024] uses insider transaction data and institutional holdings alongside tradi-
tional price and news data. The integration of such heterogeneous data sources is an
area of active development, particularly as multi-modal foundation models become
more capable.


2.2.5     Data Integration Challenges

Financial agents face several data integration challenges. First, different data sources
arrive at different frequencies—prices at millisecond to daily intervals, news irregu-
larly, and financial reports quarterly. Agents must handle this temporal misalignment.
Second, data quality varies significantly across sources, with social media data be-
ing particularly noisy. Third, the volume of available data often exceeds the context
window of LLMs, requiring agents to implement summarization, filtering, or retrieval-
augmented generation strategies to select the most relevant information.



2.3     Agent Design
This chapter presents a taxonomy of agent design patterns observed in the financial
AI literature. We categorize existing work by architectural paradigm rather than
by financial application, focusing on the structural choices that define how agents
perceive, reason, remember, and act.

                                          13
2.3.1    Single-Agent Architectures

DRL-Based Agents The simplest agent design is a single DRL agent that directly
maps market observations to trading actions. These agents formulate trading as a
Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the state includes market features, the action
space encompasses buy/hold/sell decisions with optional position sizing, and the re-
ward reflects trading performance. The FinRL library [Liu et al., 2022b,a] standard-
ized this paradigm with a three-layer architecture separating market environment,
agent training, and application layers, supporting algorithms including PPO, A2C,
DDPG, SAC, and TD3. Zhang et al. [2020] applied deep reinforcement learning with
attention mechanisms, and Sun et al. [2021] provided a comprehensive survey of RL
for quantitative trading. The key limitation of DRL-based agents is their reliance on
numerical state representations, which makes it difficult to incorporate unstructured
information such as news or financial reports without extensive feature engineering.


LLM-Based Single Agents LLM-based single agents address the limitation above
by using language models as the core reasoning engine. In this design, all financial
data—numerical, textual, and sometimes visual—is converted into prompts that the
LLM processes to generate trading decisions. The agent typically follows a perceive-
reason-act loop: it ingests current market information, reasons about it (potentially
using chain-of-thought), and outputs a trading action or signal. FinGPT [Yang et al.,
2023] represents a foundational approach, providing open-source financial LLMs fine-
tuned for sentiment analysis and trading signal generation. Rather than building a
full agent loop, FinGPT focuses on the LLM backbone, enabling downstream agent
applications. Lopez-Lira and Tang [2023] demonstrated that even zero-shot LLM
predictions based on news headlines can generate profitable trading signals, validating
the potential of language models as financial reasoning engines.




                                          14
2.3.2      Memory-Augmented Agents

A critical challenge for financial agents is maintaining relevant context over time.
Memory-augmented agent designs address this by incorporating explicit memory
structures that store, organize, and retrieve past observations and reflections.
   FinMem [Yu et al., 2023] introduced a layered memory architecture inspired by
human cognitive structures. The agent maintains three memory tiers: working mem-
ory for immediate context, episodic memory for recent events, and semantic memory
for long-term knowledge. Raw inputs such as daily news and financial reports are
summarized into memories, and upon new observations, relevant memories are re-
trieved based on recency, relevancy, and importance scores. This design allows the
agent to evolve its knowledge over time and react to new market signals with historical
context.
   FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] extended the memory-augmented paradigm with a
dual-level mechanism. It stores not only raw observations but also learned lessons
from past trading experiences, creating a self-improving feedback loop. The diversified
retrieval operation assigns specific retrieval types to historical information, enabling
the agent to access past market intelligence from multiple perspectives and senses.
   The memory-augmented design pattern is particularly important in finance be-
cause market regimes change over time, and an agent’s ability to recall and reason
about analogous historical situations can significantly improve decision quality.


2.3.3      Tool-Augmented Agents

Tool-augmented agents extend the base LLM with access to external tools and APIs,
enabling capabilities that pure language generation cannot provide. In the financial
context, tools may include real-time market data APIs, technical indicator calculators,
backtesting engines, web search for current news, code execution environments for
quantitative analysis, and order execution interfaces.
   FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] exemplifies this pattern by integrating established

                                          15
trading strategies and expert financial tools with the LLM’s reasoning. The agent can
invoke technical analysis tools, query market data APIs, and execute code to process
numerical data that would be unreliable if handled through pure language generation.
   QuantAgent [Wang et al., 2024b] takes tool augmentation further by using LLMs
not for direct trading but for generating alpha factors—quantitative signals that pre-
dict asset returns. The system operates through an inner-loop and outer-loop archi-
tecture: the inner loop uses the LLM to generate candidate alpha factor expressions,
while the outer loop validates them against historical data using backtesting tools.
This design leverages the LLM’s creative reasoning for hypothesis generation while
relying on quantitative tools for rigorous validation.
   Tool augmentation is particularly valuable in finance because financial reasoning
often requires precise numerical computation that LLMs handle poorly, and because
real-time market data access is essential for actionable decisions.


2.3.4     Reasoning-and-Reflection Agents

Reasoning-and-reflection agents incorporate explicit mechanisms for the agent to eval-
uate and improve its own reasoning process. This design pattern goes beyond simple
chain-of-thought prompting by adding iterative self-critique, reflection on past deci-
sions, and adaptive strategy refinement.
   FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] implements a dual-level reflection mechanism. At
the first level, the agent reflects on individual trading decisions by analyzing whether
the outcome matched its reasoning. At the second level, it performs meta-reflection on
its overall strategy, identifying systematic biases or recurring errors. These reflections
are stored in memory and influence future decisions.
   FinMem [Yu et al., 2023] incorporates reflection through its memory architec-
ture: when new observations arrive, the agent generates reflections that synthesize
recent memories with new information. Both memories and reflections are stored and
retrieved during subsequent decision-making, creating a cycle of experience-driven


                                           16
improvement.
   The reasoning-and-reflection pattern is critical in finance because markets are non-
stationary, and strategies that worked in one regime may fail in another. An agent’s
ability to recognize when its assumptions are no longer valid and adapt accordingly
is essential for sustained performance.


2.3.5    Multi-Agent Collaborative Frameworks

Multi-agent designs assign different specialized roles to separate LLM agents that
collaborate to produce decisions. This pattern mirrors the organizational structure
of real-world trading firms and investment institutions, where analysts, researchers,
traders, and risk managers each contribute specialized expertise.


Role-Specialized Agent Teams TradingAgents [Xiao et al., 2024] proposed a
framework directly inspired by professional trading firms, featuring agents in special-
ized roles: fundamental analysts who assess company financials, sentiment analysts
who gauge market mood from social media, technical analysts who identify price
patterns, news analysts who evaluate macroeconomic developments, and traders who
synthesize all inputs to make final decisions. The framework also includes a risk
management team that monitors portfolio exposure.
   HedgeAgents [Li et al., 2025] introduced a coordinated multi-agent system with a
central fund manager agent orchestrating multiple specialized hedging expert agents,
each focusing on different asset classes. This hierarchical design enables efficient
division of labor while maintaining overall portfolio coherence.


Debate-Driven Agent Architectures A distinctive variant of multi-agent design
uses structured debate between agents to improve reasoning quality. In TradingA-
gents [Xiao et al., 2024], Bull and Bear researcher agents independently assess market
conditions from optimistic and pessimistic perspectives, then engage in a structured


                                          17
debate. The trader agent observes this debate and synthesizes the opposing argu-
ments to make a more balanced decision. Xing [2025] applied heterogeneous LLM
agent debate to financial sentiment analysis, where agents with different biases or
perspectives analyze the same news and reach more robust classifications through
argumentation. This approach helps mitigate the tendency of individual LLMs to
exhibit overconfident or biased sentiment predictions.


Information-Distillation Agent Networks FINCON [Yu et al., 2024] proposed
a multi-agent system with conceptual verbal reinforcement, addressing the challenge
of information overload in financial decision-making. Rather than passing all raw
data between agents, FINCON implements an information distillation process where
each agent compresses and refines information before passing it to downstream agents.
This addresses a key weakness of naive multi-agent systems—the “telephone effect”
where details are lost and states become corrupted as information passes through
multiple agents.


2.3.6      Hybrid Agent Designs

In practice, many state-of-the-art financial agents combine multiple design patterns.
FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] is simultaneously memory-augmented, tool-augmented,
reasoning-and-reflection-enabled, and multimodal. TradingAgents [Xiao et al., 2024]
combines multi-agent collaboration with role specialization and debate mechanisms.
This trend toward hybrid designs reflects the inherent complexity of financial decision-
making, which demands diverse capabilities that no single design pattern can fully
address.
   An important design consideration in hybrid systems is the coordination overhead.
As agents become more complex, the computational cost of inference increases, the
number of potential failure modes grows, and the system becomes harder to debug and
interpret. Balancing architectural sophistication with practical deployability remains


                                          18
an open challenge.



2.4        Applications
This chapter organizes the literature by financial application domain, examining how
the agent design patterns described in Section 2.3 are deployed to address specific
financial tasks.


2.4.1      Trading

Trading is the most extensively studied application for financial AI agents. The goal
is to generate buy/sell/hold signals or execute trades that maximize risk-adjusted
returns.


Stock Trading Stock trading has served as the primary testbed for both DRL-
based and LLM-based agents. FinRL [Liu et al., 2022b,a] demonstrated that DRL
agents (PPO, DDPG, A2C) can outperform traditional strategies on Dow Jones 30
constituents and S&P 500 stocks. LLM-based agents have extended this by incorpo-
rating news and sentiment: FinMem [Yu et al., 2023] showed that layered memory
with character design improves trading decisions on individual stocks, while FinAgent
[Zhang et al., 2024] achieved an average 36% improvement in profitability across six
datasets by integrating multimodal data. Multi-agent systems have pushed perfor-
mance further. TradingAgents [Xiao et al., 2024] demonstrated significant improve-
ments in cumulative returns, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown by simulating
the collaborative dynamics of a professional trading firm. FINCON [Yu et al., 2024]
showed superior performance over both DRL agents and single LLM agents through
its information distillation approach.


Cryptocurrency Trading Cryptocurrency markets, characterized by high volatil-
ity, 24/7 operation, and strong social media influence, represent a distinct challenge

                                         19
for trading agents. FinAgent [Zhang et al., 2024] and FinRL [Liu et al., 2022b]
both include cryptocurrency datasets in their evaluations. The unique dynamics of
crypto markets—including the importance of on-chain data, social media hype cycles,
and regulatory uncertainty—make them a particularly interesting testbed for agent
adaptability.


Algorithmic and High-Frequency Trading While most LLM-based agents op-
erate at daily or intraday frequencies, algorithmic and high-frequency trading (HFT)
remain primarily the domain of DRL agents and traditional quantitative systems due
to the latency constraints of LLM inference. However, LLM agents are increasingly
used for longer-horizon components of algorithmic strategies, such as alpha factor
generation [Wang et al., 2024b] and regime detection, while faster numerical models
handle execution.


2.4.2     Portfolio Management

Portfolio management extends beyond individual asset trading to the construction
and rebalancing of multi-asset portfolios.
   DRL-based portfolio management agents optimize asset allocation by treating the
portfolio weight vector as the action space. FinRL [Liu et al., 2022b] supports portfo-
lio allocation across multiple stocks with algorithms that learn to balance return and
risk. LLM-based approaches to portfolio management are emerging: FINCON [Yu
et al., 2024] evaluated its multi-agent system on portfolio management tasks com-
paring against Markowitz mean-variance optimization and equal-weighted strategies.
HedgeAgents [Li et al., 2025] addressed multi-asset hedging through a hierarchical
multi-agent design where specialized agents manage different asset classes while a
central manager ensures overall portfolio coherence. This mirrors real-world asset
management organizations where sector specialists report to a chief investment offi-
cer.


                                             20
2.4.3    Risk Management and Fraud Detection

Fraud Detection Traditional fraud detection relies on rule-based filters and super-
vised classifiers. Agent-based approaches enhance these with autonomous monitoring,
adaptive learning, and multi-step reasoning [Hilal et al., 2022]. LLM-powered fraud
detection agents can process unstructured data such as customer communications
and news alongside structured transaction data, providing a more holistic view of
potential fraud patterns [Dong et al., 2025]. The agentic approach enables systems
that dynamically adjust detection thresholds, correlate suspicious activities across
accounts, and generate human-readable explanations for flagged transactions.


Credit Risk Assessment AI agents for credit risk go beyond traditional scoring by
incorporating dynamic information gathering and multi-step reasoning. LLM-based
agents can analyze financial statements, assess qualitative risk factors from manage-
ment discussions, and integrate macroeconomic indicators [Kim et al., 2024]. The
agentic design enables systems that actively seek additional information when initial
assessments are uncertain and provide transparent reasoning chains for regulatory
compliance.


Market Risk Monitoring Multi-agent systems have been applied to market risk
monitoring, where different agents track different risk dimensions—market risk, credit
risk, liquidity risk, and operational risk—and coordinate their assessments. The risk
management team in TradingAgents [Xiao et al., 2024] exemplifies this approach,
monitoring portfolio exposure and intervening when risk thresholds are breached.


2.4.4    Financial Analysis and Advisory

Financial Report Analysis LLM agents have demonstrated strong capabilities
in analyzing financial reports. Kim et al. [2024] showed that GPT-4 can generate
earnings predictions rivaling professional analysts. The agent-based extension in-

Sentiment-Driven Analysis Sentiment analysis agents process news, social me-

Robo-Advisory AI agents for financial advisory combine financial knowledge with
personalized client profiling. These systems assess risk tolerance, financial goals, and

2.5        Benchmarks and Evaluation

2.5.1      Trading Performance Metrics

Financial AI agents are typically evaluated using standard quantitative finance met-
rics. Cumulative Return (CR) measures total return over the evaluation period.
Sharpe Ratio (SR) captures risk-adjusted return as excess return per unit of volatil-
ity. Maximum Drawdown (MDD) measures the largest peak-to-trough decline, quan-
tifying downside risk. Additional metrics include the Sortino Ratio (considering only
downside deviation) and the Calmar Ratio (annualized return divided by maximum
drawdown).


2.5.2      Financial NLP Benchmarks

For agents that process textual data, several benchmarks evaluate component capa-
bilities. FinBen [Xie et al., 2024] provides a holistic benchmark covering sentiment
analysis, named entity recognition, question answering, and numerical reasoning over



                                          23
financial data. FinanceBench [Islam et al., 2023] focuses on question answering over
real financial documents including SEC filings.


2.5.3      Agent-Level Benchmarks

A critical gap in the current literature is the lack of comprehensive agent-level bench-
marks that evaluate end-to-end task completion in realistic financial scenarios. While
individual systems are evaluated on their specific tasks, standardized benchmarks that
enable fair comparison across different agent architectures remain scarce. Emerging
efforts such as FinAgentBench [Choi et al., 2025] aim to address this by evaluating
agents on multi-step financial tasks that require information gathering, reasoning,
and action.


2.5.4      Evaluation Challenges

Several challenges complicate the evaluation of financial AI agents. First, backtesting
on historical data may overestimate performance due to look-ahead bias, survivorship
bias, and the inability to account for market impact. Second, live trading evaluation is
expensive and risky. Third, comparing agents across different papers is difficult due
to variations in datasets, time periods, transaction cost assumptions, and baseline
configurations [Liu et al., 2022a]. Fourth, many LLM-based agents use proprietary
models (e.g., GPT-4), making exact reproduction challenging. Fifth, financial mar-
kets are non-stationary, so strong historical performance does not guarantee future
results.

Research Methodology

3.1     Gap Analysis
Existing work on LLM-based financial reasoning falls into two camps that address dif-
ferent parts of the problem. The first camp, exemplified by TradingAgents [Xiao et al.,
2024], FinCon [Yu et al., 2024], and FinGPT [Yang et al., 2023], builds multi-agent
systems that generate equity-level trading signals from news sentiment, technical indi-
cators, and fundamental data. These systems operate primarily at the individual asset
level; they do not, for instance, trace how a Federal Reserve rate decision propagates
through the credit channel to affect corporate bond spreads or equity risk premia.
The second camp, including EconAgent [Li et al., 2024] and Lopez-Lira and Tang
[2023], applies LLMs to macroeconomic simulation or forecasting but is not directly
connected to calibrated cross-asset allocation signals. MarketSenseAI [Fatouros et al.,
2025], while primarily an equity analysis agent for S&P 100 stocks, similarly operates
without explicit macro transmission-mechanism reasoning or cross-asset calibration.
   Three gaps motivate the design of Macro-MOSAIC. First, we are not aware of
a system that generates explicit, auditable multi-school parallel reasoning
chains where structurally distinct economic frameworks (Keynesian, Austrian, Mon-
etarist) independently analyze the same macro event and produce divergent causal


                                          25
chains that are then formally aggregated. FOMC In Silico [Kazinnik and Sinclair,
2025] comes closest by assigning dovish/hawkish personas to FOMC member agents,
but these represent individual viewpoints on a single policy variable (the federal funds
rate) rather than structurally distinct theoretical frameworks applied to general macro
events. Second, historical analogy retrieval at the macroeconomic episode
level—combining semantic similarity over event narratives with structural similarity
over macro state vectors—has not, to our knowledge, been addressed in the systems we
surveyed. Existing financial RAG systems [Zhang et al., 2024, Lithgow-Serrano et al.,
2025] retrieve similar price patterns or sentiment-level few-shot examples, not struc-
tured narratives of comparable macro crises with documented transmission mecha-
nisms and multi-asset outcomes. Third, the literature lacks a verified end-to-end
pipeline connecting qualitative macro event interpretation through calibrated prob-
ability distributions to cross-asset allocation signals, evaluated against both reasoning
benchmarks and realized financial outcomes.

3.3     Macro-MOSAIC: System Architecture
We propose Macro-MOSAIC (Mixture-Of-Schools Analogy-Informed Chains), a
four-stage pipeline that bridges qualitative macroeconomic reasoning and quantitative
asset allocation through explicit causal chain construction.


3.3.1    Stage 1: Historical Analogy Retrieval

When a new macro event e arrives, we retrieve the m most relevant historical episodes
from a curated database of 141 macro episodes spanning 1971–2025 (see Section 4.1).
Each episode is a structured record containing the triggering event, prevailing macro
context (35 FRED indicators), tagged transmission channels, multi-horizon asset re-
actions across 22 instruments, and school-specific interpretive narratives.
   Retrieval operates via a dual-channel scoring mechanism. The semantic channel
computes cosine similarity between the embedding of the incoming event description
and the pre-computed embeddings of each episode’s narrative text. The structural
channel computes Mahalanobis distance between the current macro state vector s
and each episode’s recorded macro context si , capturing similarity in the economic
regime (rate environment, inflation level, credit conditions) rather than surface-level



                                          27
narrative similarity. The final retrieval score is a weighted combination:

3.3.2     Stage 2: Multi-School Causal Chain Generation

Each retrieved analogy is interpreted through K = 3 distinct economic school per-
spectives (Keynesian, Austrian, and Monetarist), implemented as separate LoRA
adapters [Hu et al., 2022] fine-tuned on school-specific corpora. These three schools
were selected for three reasons. First, they represent structurally distinct theoretical
frameworks with genuinely different transmission mechanism logic: Keynesian anal-
ysis centers on aggregate demand and fiscal multipliers, Austrian analysis on capital
structure distortions and malinvestment, and Monetarist analysis on money supply
dynamics and expectations—producing divergent causal chains for the same macro
event. Second, these three schools are prominently represented in the academic and
policy discourse relevant to macro-financial analysis: Keynesian frameworks appear
in central bank communications and fiscal policy debates, Austrian frameworks pro-
vide a contrasting supply-side and capital-structure lens, and Monetarist frameworks
anchor inflation and monetary transmission analysis. Their perspectives are therefore
directly relevant to cross-asset signal generation even if none can be characterised as
universally dominant in institutional practice. Third, they have sufficient published
literature (NBER working papers filterable by JEL codes E12, B53, E41/E51) to con-
struct school-specific fine-tuning corpora of adequate size and quality. Post-Keynesian
and Institutionalist perspectives, while intellectually valuable, were excluded due to
smaller and less standardized corpora, which we leave as a direction for future exten-


                                             28
sion.
   The expected benefit of aggregating diverse school-specific forecasts rests on a
standard ensemble learning argument: if individual school models make different
systematic errors—for instance, Keynesian frameworks tend to underweight supply-
side transmission channels while Austrian frameworks tend to underweight demand-
multiplier effects—their errors will partially offset under aggregation, reducing the
ensemble’s overall systematic bias. This argument requires two empirical conditions
to hold. First, each school must achieve above-chance directional accuracy on at least
a subset of event categories, so that its contribution to the ensemble is informative
rather than noise. Second, the school-specific outputs must be genuinely distinct
rather than stylistic variations of a single mainstream analysis. Our preliminary
results in Section 4.3 provide partial evidence on both conditions: Table 4.7 (Sec-
tion 4.3.5) shows that the Monetarist adapter produces the largest consistency gains
on inflation and employment events while gains are smallest for geopolitical shocks,
suggesting category-level complementarity across schools; Table 4.4 shows that fine-
tuned adapters achieve a mean inter-school Jaccard distance of 0.853 versus 0.778 for
the base model, confirming signal-level diversity. Whether these Stage 2 properties
translate into improved calibration scores and risk-adjusted returns in the end-to-
end pipeline remains an open empirical question, which the ablation experiments in
Section 5.4 are specifically designed to isolate and test.

4.3.1       School Consistency

Fine-tuning produces substantial gains in school-specific reasoning for the Austrian
and Monetarist agents, with more modest results for the Keynesian agent.
   The Monetarist adapter achieves the largest improvement, with a consistency
score of 0.528 compared to 0.230 for the base model (+129.7%). The SFT agent
wins on 80 of 96 events (83.3%). Examining the sub-components, we find that the
SFT Monetarist agent hits 3.6 out of 6 must-have keywords per response (vs. 2.6
for base) and 2.4 strong-signal keywords (vs. 1.4). The base model rarely produces
core Monetarist terms like MV=PQ, quantity theory, or long and variable lags un-
less explicitly prompted, whereas the fine-tuned agent incorporates these concepts as


                                            40
natural components of its reasoning chain.
   The Austrian adapter shows similarly large relative improvement (+118.8%),
rising from 0.146 to 0.319, with SFT winning on 75.0% of events. The base model
scores particularly low on Austrian must-have terms (0.3 out of 6), indicating that
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct has limited familiarity with heterodox Austrian concepts such as
malinvestment, ABCT, and capital structure distortion. Fine-tuning raises must-have
hits to 1.9 out of 6, demonstrating successful internalization of these domain-specific
concepts.
   The Keynesian adapter shows a small decrease in the composite consistency score
(−8.1%), which warrants careful interpretation. The apparent regression is an artifact
of the scoring metric, not a degradation in reasoning quality: the deficit is driven en-
tirely by reduced strong-signal keyword hits, while school purity and output structure
both improve substantially. Decomposing the score reveals that the deficit is driven
entirely by a reduction in strong-signal hits (2.1 → 0.9), while must-have keyword
coverage improves (3.0 → 3.4 out of 7), anti-signal contamination drops to zero (0.44
→ 0.00), and structured output block presence rises from 59% to 97%. We attribute
the strong-signal decline to a fundamental asymmetry between the Keynesian school
and the other two: the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct base model has been pretrained on large
volumes of mainstream macroeconomic text in which Keynesian vocabulary is the
dominant analytical idiom. The base model therefore produces stylistically Keyne-
sian outputs without fine-tuning, but its fluency is undifferentiated—conflating New
Keynesian IS-LM reasoning with Post-Keynesian and Old Keynesian terminology into
a single vocabulary pool. Fine-tuning specializes the adapter toward New Keynesian
axiomatic structure, causing it to avoid Post-Keynesian strong-signal terms (e.g., en-
dogenous money, Minsky moment) that fall outside this sub-school’s scope—reducing
keyword count while improving theoretical coherence. For the downstream pipeline,
the properties that matter are output parsability and school purity, not standalone
keyword density: the SFT Keynesian adapter’s 97% structured output rate (vs. 59%


                                          41
for base) and perfect anti-signal elimination make it substantially more reliable as an
input to the Bayesian aggregation stage than the base model’s superficially higher
consistency score would suggest.

5.2     Stage 1: Retrieval-Augmented Analogy En-
        gine
Embedding and indexing. We will encode all 141 episode narratives and their
associated macro context vectors into a dual-index retrieval store. For the seman-
tic channel, we will embed each episode’s embedding text field using a sentence
transformer (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2 or BGE-large-en) and store the vectors in a
FAISS index with inner-product search. For the structural channel, we will normal-
ize the 35-dimensional FRED macro context vectors across all episodes, compute
the pooled covariance matrix Σ, and implement Mahalanobis distance retrieval via
             p
dM (s, si ) = (s − si )⊤ Σ−1 (s − si ). Missing values in the macro context (primarily
for pre-1990 episodes where certain FRED series do not exist) will be handled via
mean imputation within each decade.


Retrieval fusion and evaluation. The semantic and structural scores will be
combined via the weighted score in Eq. (3.1), with α tuned on a held-out validation
set of 20 episodes. We will evaluate retrieval quality through two protocols: (i) a
category-match metric measuring whether the top-3 retrieved episodes share at least
one category tag with the query episode (e.g., a banking crisis retrieves other banking
crises), and (ii) a downstream utility metric measuring whether including the retrieved
analogies improves the school-specific causal chain quality compared to no-retrieval

baselines, scored by human or GPT-4o evaluation on a 1–5 rubric for causal coherence,
mechanism specificity, and asset prediction accuracy.


Estimated timeline. 1–2 weeks. The primary engineering effort is building the
dual-index retrieval pipeline and tuning α; no model training is required.



5.3      Stage 3: Bayesian Aggregation and Calibra-
         tion
Calibration data collection. We will collect resolved prediction market questions
from Polymarket (via public API and Kaggle dumps) and Metaculus (via the Python
SDK at github.com/Metaculus/forecasting-tools), filtering for macroeconomic
and geopolitical topics (monetary policy, inflation, GDP, elections, conflicts). We
target a minimum of 500 resolved question–resolution pairs spanning 2023–2025 for
calibration training and 200 held-out pairs for evaluation.


School-specific Brier Score tracking. For each resolved question, we will run
all three LoRA adapters to generate predicted probabilities, then compute per-school
Brier Scores partitioned by macro regime. Regime labels will be assigned by fitting
a 3-state Gaussian HMM (using hmmlearn) on monthly S&P 500 returns and real-
ized volatility, producing states interpretable as expansion, contraction, and crisis.
The regime-conditional Brier Scores will populate the dynamic weighting function in
Eq. (3.3).


Calibration layer training. We will implement and compare three calibration
methods: Platt scaling (logistic, 2 parameters), isotonic regression (non-parametric),
and temperature scaling [Guo et al., 2017] (1 parameter). Each will be trained on the
500-question training set and evaluated on the 200-question held-out set using Brier
Score, Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 bins), and reliability diagrams. We will

                                         49
report whether the two-stage process (regime-conditional weighting → Platt scaling)
outperforms single-stage calibration applied directly to equal-weighted aggregates.


Estimated timeline. 2–3 weeks. The bottleneck is running all three adapters
across 700+ questions and iterating on the calibration pipeline.



5.4      Stage 4: Asset Allocation Signal Generation
         and Backtesting
Signal construction. We will implement the fractional Kelly allocation from Eq. (3.6)
with f = 0.25 and wmax = 0.10 per asset. The system will produce signals for 8
liquid instruments: S&P 500 futures (ES), 10-year Treasury futures (ZN), gold fu-
tures (GC), WTI crude oil futures (CL), DXY index, EUR/USD, USD/JPY, and the
iShares MSCI Emerging Markets ETF (EEM). Transaction costs will be modeled at
5 basis points per round trip for futures and 10 basis points for ETFs.


Backtesting protocol. Following the evaluation critique of Li et al. [2026] and
walk-forward validation standards [López de Prado, 2018], we will adopt a strictly
out-of-sample evaluation design:

    In-sample period (2015–2022): Used for calibration layer training, regime
      model fitting, and retrieval parameter tuning (α, f , λ). No signal-quality met-
      rics will be reported on this period.

    Out-of-sample period (2023–2025): All reported Sharpe ratios, drawdowns,
      and directional accuracy are computed on this period only, using a rolling 6-
      month calibration window that expands forward.

    Memorization defense: Following Lopez-Lira et al. [2025], we will include
      a pre/post knowledge-cutoff performance comparison and apply counterfactual

                                          50
      perturbation tests (randomly shifting macro input values by ±10% and verifying
      that model predictions change proportionally rather than remaining anchored
      to memorized outputs).

    Fine-tuning isolation: LoRA adapter training relies on synthetic trajecto-
      ries generated from abstract templates with randomized macro states, not on
      the historical episode records themselves. No instance-level information about
      specific 2023–2025 events enters the fine-tuning corpora, ensuring that the out-
      of-sample evaluation window is unseen at the adapter training stage.


Baselines. We will compare Macro-MOSAIC signals against five baselines: (i) buy-
and-hold S&P 500, (ii) a random walk forecast (no-change assumption), (iii) a Bayesian
VAR model (BVAR with Minnesota prior) on the same 8 instruments, (iv) GPT-4o
with zero-shot CoT prompting given the same event inputs, and (v) the single best-
performing LoRA adapter (no multi-school aggregation). Pairwise forecast compar-
isons will use Diebold-Mariano tests with Newey-West standard errors.


Metrics. Annualized Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, Sortino ratio, annualized
return, directional accuracy (fraction of trades with correct sign), and CAPM alpha.
Statistical significance of Sharpe differences will be assessed via the Ledoit and Wolf
[2008] test.


Estimated timeline. 2–3 weeks. The primary effort is building the event-driven
backtesting harness and ensuring no look-ahead bias in the signal generation pipeline.

5.7      Risk, Governance, and Auditability
Deploying an LLM-based system in a financial decision context raises several risks
that we address explicitly here.


Hallucination and factual grounding. LLMs can generate plausible but factu-
ally incorrect causal chains. Macro-MOSAIC mitigates this through two structural
mechanisms: (i) retrieved historical episodes provide concrete anchor points—specific
dates, FRED values, and measured asset reactions—that constrain generation, and
(ii) the structured chain schema requires each step to name a specific, verifiable trans-
mission mechanism (e.g., credit channel, exchange rate channel) rather than vague
narrative. Causal claims are therefore traceable to specific evidence in the episode
database. Residual hallucination risk will be quantified in the EconCausal evaluation



                                           53
(Section 5.4), which compares generated causal steps against a ground-truth causal
triplet database.


Data leakage and reproducibility. As discussed in Section 5.3, LoRA adapters
are trained on synthetic trajectories generated from abstract templates with ran-
domised macro snapshots; no historical episode records from the out-of-sample pe-
riod (2023–2025) enter the fine-tuning corpora. The episode database construction
pipeline will be fully documented and released, including data sources (Bloomberg,
Yahoo Finance, FRED), extraction scripts, and the LLM prompts used to generate
narrative summaries and school interpretations, enabling independent replication.


Backtest overfitting. Walk-forward evaluation with a rolling 6-month calibration
window and strictly out-of-sample reporting (Section 5.3) limits look-ahead bias. In
addition, the −RAG and −Multi-school ablations (Section 5.4) test whether perfor-
mance gains are attributable to the structural components or to incidental pattern
matching. We will apply the Ledoit-Wolf Sharpe ratio test and report bootstrap
confidence intervals to guard against inflated significance claims.


Human oversight. Macro-MOSAIC is designed as a decision-support system, not
an autonomous trading agent. Each output is a structured record containing the
retrieved analogies, explicit causal chains, scenario probabilities, and per-asset con-
viction scores, all of which a human portfolio manager can inspect and override. The
explicit school labels and transmission mechanism fields make the system’s reasoning
auditable in a way that black-box LLM outputs are not. In any live deployment, final
allocation decisions would remain under human review, with the system’s role limited
to structured signal generation.


Traceability. The causal chain schema (event → mechanism → intermediate effects
→ asset impacts) ensures that every allocation signal can be traced back through the


                                          54
probability aggregation to the school-specific chains, and from those chains to the re-
trieved historical episodes and the specific FRED values and price reactions that mo-
tivate the analogy. This traceability is an explicit design objective of Macro-MOSAIC
and distinguishes it from systems that produce opaque end-to-end predictions.
